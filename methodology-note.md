# Methodology Note

## Scope

This project is a **conceptual policy-design and decision-architecture prototype**, not an impact evaluation and not an empirical classification of territorial public self-government (TOS) organizations.

The public version is intentionally curated from a more detailed academic policy-design model. It retains the transferable decision logic while omitting internal coding systems, granular measure registries, numerical weights, implementation-hour estimates, confidential research-project information, and other material unnecessary for understanding the framework.

## Case application

The case application is Territorial Public Self-Government (TOS) in the Voronezh Region, Russia.

TOS is treated as a form of resident self-organization within the broader system of local self-government. Regional actors are modeled as potential designers of support conditions, resources, coordination arrangements, and enabling infrastructure—not as managers of community self-governance bodies.

## Design problem

The model starts from a simple problem:

> Similar visible symptoms may have different causes and therefore should not automatically trigger identical policy responses.

A small active core, for example, may reflect restricted access, but it may also reflect voluntary delegation, specialization, task cycles, or a lack of demand for additional roles.

## Decision architecture

The public framework follows this sequence:

1. **Diagnosis** — describe the starting condition without collapsing it into a single score.
2. **Competing explanations** — retain alternative mechanisms until evidence distinguishes them.
3. **Safeguard check** — test whether a proposed intervention is admissible.
4. **Conditional pathway** — choose a proportionate response linked to the mechanism being tested.
5. **Resource fit** — match intervention scope to capacity and reversibility.
6. **Checkpoint** — reassess the diagnosis using new evidence.
7. **Adaptive decision** — continue, modify, switch, pause, maintain, or stop.

## Methods used

The academic model combines:

- literature synthesis;
- legal and institutional analysis;
- problem decomposition;
- theory-of-change logic;
- backcasting;
- scenario and conditional-pathway design;
- adaptive decision logic;
- explicit treatment of uncertainty;
- sensitivity testing of internal normative assumptions.

## Uncertainty rules

The framework preserves several distinctions that are easy to lose in administrative scoring systems:

- unknown is not zero;
- no data is not evidence of poor performance;
- no visible activity is not the same as confirmed inactivity;
- no observed request is not proof of absent demand when access has not been established as genuinely open;
- a successful pilot in one context is not sufficient evidence for automatic scaling elsewhere.

## Safeguards

The public version uses five non-compensatory safeguards:

1. lawful authority;
2. voluntariness and autonomy;
3. meaningful influence and accountability;
4. adequate rights, support, and resources;
5. evidentiary integrity.

A safeguard failure cannot be offset by a high aggregate priority score or a large number of activities.

## Weighting and sensitivity

The full academic model distinguishes **normative importance** from **context-specific priority** and includes sensitivity checks.

These weights are authorial design calibrations. They are not regional statistics, stakeholder-agreed weights, expert-Delphi results, or empirical estimates of causal contribution.

The public v1.1 interface therefore does not expose the numerical weighting architecture.

## Limitations

The public prototype does not establish the prevalence of its illustrative diagnostic configurations in the Voronezh Region and does not claim that the displayed pathways are empirically validated interventions.

Any real-world use would require context-specific evidence, applicable legal review, appropriate local consent, implementation capacity, and empirical evaluation.
